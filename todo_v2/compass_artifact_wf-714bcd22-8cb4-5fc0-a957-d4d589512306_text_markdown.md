# Membrane Lipid Composition as a Determinant of Kinase Inhibitor Partitioning, Binding, and Activity

## TL;DR
- **Membrane lipid composition is a first-order, often underappreciated pharmacological variable for kinase inhibitors:** because most kinase inhibitors are lipophilic weak bases with high cLogP, they partition heavily into bilayers (an ITC-measured POPC membrane–water constant of ~84,000 M⁻¹ for lapatinib), which lowers the free concentration available to engage the ATP site, concentrates drug near membrane-proximal targets, and creates systematic gaps between biochemical and cellular potency.
- **The effect is composition-dependent and bidirectional:** cholesterol, acyl-chain order, and anionic headgroups (PS/PI/PIP2/cardiolipin) tune both where a drug sits in the bilayer and how strongly it binds—electrostatics amplify binding of cationic species (~7–8-fold with PS in model systems) but *reduce* it for neutral lipophiles; cholesterol-rich rafts sequester EGFR/HER2 and confer inhibitor resistance that statins/cholesterol depletion can reverse.
- **Documented efficacy/resistance consequences are real but mechanism-specific:** lysosomotropic sequestration (sunitinib, nintedanib), raft/cholesterol-dependent RTK resistance, P-gp "hydrophobic vacuum cleaner" efflux from the inner leaflet, and drug-induced phospholipidosis are all tied to membrane partitioning—though the field still debates how much lysosomal trapping *alone* drives clinical resistance.

## Key Findings

1. **Physicochemistry predisposes kinase inhibitors to membrane partitioning.** Typical small-molecule kinase inhibitors are lipophilic, weakly basic amines with high cLogP. Lipophilic weak bases have high affinity for the acidic phospholipids that constitute biological membranes and therefore tend toward high volumes of distribution (Rodgers & Rowland tissue-composition models, J Pharm Sci 2005/2006). Membranes act as a two-dimensional reservoir/concentrator, reducing unbound cytosolic drug available for target engagement.

2. **Partition coefficients into real bilayers substantially exceed and diverge from octanol-based estimates.** For lapatinib, an ITC-measured POPC membrane–water partition constant of K(0) = 84 mM⁻¹ (≈84,000 M⁻¹; log K ≈ 4.9) was reported by Naßwetter/Scheidt et al. (*Biophysical Chemistry* 2021, 277:106654) using a chaotropic-cosolvent/DMSO-extrapolation method (slope m = 28 kJ/mol vs DMSO), building on Haralampiev et al. (*BBA Biomembranes* 2020;1862:183414). Crucially, this real membrane value diverges systematically from logP-derived estimates—evidence that octanol/water logP is an unreliable surrogate for bilayer partitioning.

3. **Headgroup charge governs the *direction* of composition effects.** Anionic phospholipids electrostatically enhance binding of cationic/basic ligands (PS titration monotonically raises Kp of basic drugs, Omran et al., Chem Pharm Bull 2002; a cationic peptide showed ~7–8-fold higher Kp in PC/PS vs PC), but for the neutral lipophile lapatinib, adding anionic lipids or cholesterol *decreased* the partition constant to ~100 M⁻¹ (Haralampiev et al. 2020). The generalization "anionic lipids increase membrane binding" holds only for the protonated/cationic species; it fails for non-basic lipophiles, where cholesterol-induced acyl-chain ordering reduces uptake.

4. **Membrane/intracellular binding lowers free drug and explains biochemical-vs-cellular potency gaps.** Unbound intracellular concentration (Cu,cell) and the unbound partition coefficient (Kpuu) determine target exposure. For a panel of KAT6A inhibitors in ZR75-1 breast cancer cells, cellular/biochemical potency drop-off ranged 1.3- to 260-fold, and a physiologically based saturable-Kpuu approach bridged the *average* exposure gap from 73-fold down to only 5.4-fold (Trünkle et al., *Drug Metab Dispos* 2020;48(7):553–562). The intracellular-bioavailability (Fic = fu,cell × Kp) framework of Mateus et al. (*PNAS* 2017;114:E6231) demonstrated that kinase-inhibitor cellular potency loss reflects not only high cellular ATP but also limited intracellular target *access*.

5. **Membrane-proximal kinase targets make localization mechanistically central.** EGFR/ErbB (transmembrane + juxtamembrane), RAF at the RAS–membrane interface, PI3K acting on PIP2/PIP3, BTK recruited via its PH domain to PIP3 (and PS), and ABL's myristoyl pocket (asciminib/GNF-2) are all membrane-coupled. Acidic membrane lipids drive EGFR juxtamembrane dimerization; RAF inhibitors (vemurafenib/dabrafenib) paradoxically promote RAS-dependent membrane recruitment and dimerization of wild-type RAF.

6. **Drugs act as bilayer modifiers ("membrane catalysis"/off-target promiscuity).** Amphiphilic drugs adsorb at the bilayer interface and change bilayer properties (thickness, elasticity, curvature/lateral-pressure profile), indirectly altering embedded protein function. Ingólfsson & Andersen's gramicidin-based fluorescence/single-channel assays quantify this bilayer-modifying propensity as a mechanism of nonspecific "off-target" effects.

7. **Cholesterol/raft biology drives RTK inhibitor resistance.** Lipid-raft localization of EGFR correlates with TKI resistance, and cholesterol depletion (lovastatin, MβCD) sensitizes resistant cells to gefitinib synergistically.

8. **Lysosomotropism and phospholipidosis are membrane-partitioning phenomena** for hydrophobic weak-base and cationic amphiphilic TKIs, with both efficacy (drug scavenging) and toxicology (phospholipidosis) consequences.

## Details

### 1. Molecular Mechanisms

**Physicochemistry and the reservoir concept.** Kinase inhibitors are predominantly lipophilic, weakly basic molecules. Their neutral forms diffuse freely across membranes; upon reaching acidic compartments (lysosomes/late endosomes, pH ~4.5–5) they protonate and are trapped ("ion trapping"). In bulk cell/tissue terms, lipophilic weak bases bind avidly to acidic phospholipids and neutral lipids, producing large volumes of distribution. The Rodgers & Rowland tissue-composition models formalized this: the dominant affinity of the charged species of basic drugs (pKa > 7) is to tissue acidic phospholipids, with the interaction affinity estimable from blood-cell binding. Predictions for bases are less accurate than for acids/neutrals, indicating additional processes; a later extension (DMD 2021;49:53) added explicit lysosomal trapping using the lysosomal marker BMP, improving Kpu predictions for lysosome-rich tissues (lysosomal volume fraction 0.03% adipose → 5.3% spleen; e.g., nicotine Kpu +4-fold in spleen).

**Partitioning thermodynamics and localization.** Lipid bilayers are amphipathic multilayered structures and do not behave like bulk octanol; amphipathic drugs concentrate in the interfacial (headgroup/glycerol backbone) region rather than the hydrophobic core. IAM-HPLC and liposome partition studies show that protonation of basic compounds does *not* appreciably reduce phospholipid partitioning (unlike octanol/water), consistent with the "pH-piston" mechanism, in which cationic groups interact attractively with the phosphate moieties. For lapatinib, ITC gave a POPC membrane–water K ≈ 84 mM⁻¹, with deeper embedding for lapatinib (logP ≈ 6.45) than tofacitinib (logP ≈ 0.45); lapatinib disturbs bilayer structure and increases permeability to polar molecules (Haralampiev et al., BBA Biomembranes 2020). The kinase inhibitor ceritinib—but not imatinib—significantly disturbs lipid membrane integrity (Fischer et al., "The small-molecule kinase inhibitor ceritinib, unlike imatinib, causes a significant disturbance of lipid membrane integrity," *Chem Phys Lipids* 2023;257:105351, PMID 37863350), illustrating structure-dependent bilayer perturbation within a single drug class.

**Headgroup charge and dipole potential.** Anionic lipids (PS, PG, PI, PIP2, cardiolipin) electrostatically recruit protonated basic drugs at the interface. PS titration increases Kp of basic drugs (diazepam, flurazepam) monotonically (Omran et al., Chem Pharm Bull 2002); cationic MDR modulators such as verapamil and phenothiazines bind more strongly to anionic membranes and can compete with doxorubicin for membrane partitioning (Wesołowska/Hendrich, Biophys Chem). Membrane dipole potential (tens of mV) and its modulation by adsorbed amphiphiles influence the depth and orientation of charged species. The lapatinib counter-example (K falling to ~100 M⁻¹ with anionic lipids/cholesterol) demonstrates the charge dependence: for a non-cationic lipophile the electrostatic enhancement is absent and cholesterol-induced ordering reduces partitioning.

**Membrane as a free-concentration buffer.** Because only unbound drug engages the target, extensive membrane/microsomal partitioning means the unbound (free) concentration can be far below the total. Microsomal binding (fum) correlates with logP/logD (Austin linear; Hallifax quadratic relationships), and the general lack of species differences supports partitioning into phospholipid rather than binding to microsomal protein—reinforcing that "nominal" assay concentration ≠ free ≠ membrane-bound concentration.

**Membrane-proximal and membrane-embedded kinase targets.**
- *EGFR/ErbB:* Acidic membrane lipids induce EGFR juxtamembrane (JM-A) dimerization by increasing JM affinity for the membrane; Thr654 phosphorylation electrostatically dissociates the JM dimer by disrupting interactions with acidic lipids (single-molecule FRET/nanodisc work, JBC 2018). Cholesterol dictates the lateral mobility ("freedom") of EGFR/HER2 in the plane of the membrane (Orr et al., Biophys J 2005). Raft localization alters inhibitor response (see §3).
- *RAF/RAS:* Active RAS recruits cytosolic inactive RAF to the plasma membrane and induces dimerization. Type I/I½ ATP-competitive RAF inhibitors (vemurafenib, dabrafenib) paradoxically enhance RAS–RAF association and dimerization of wild-type RAF, an effect requiring inhibitor binding at the ATP site *and* the RAS-on membrane state; vemurafenib/PLX4720 have comparatively marginal impact on RAF autoinhibition and membrane recruitment relative to other chemotypes (Nat Commun 2017; eLife/bioRxiv 2025).
- *PI3K/BTK:* PI3K phosphorylates membrane PIP2 to generate PIP3; BTK's PH-TH module binds PIP3 and dimerizes on the membrane in a switch-like manner (fourth-order in PIP3 surface density; PNAS 2016). A two-step model adds low-affinity, high-copy-number PS for basal membrane recruitment that then sensitizes BTK to PIP3 activation (PNAS 2023)—directly linking anionic-lipid composition to signaling amplitude and, by extension, to the substrate/target context an inhibitor must compete within.
- *ABL myristoyl pocket:* Asciminib (ABL001) and the earlier GNF-2/GNF-5 bind the C-lobe myristoyl pocket normally occupied by the myristoylated N-terminus—an allosteric, lipid-mimetic ("STAMP": Specifically Targeting the ABL Myristoyl Pocket) site (Schoepfer et al., J Med Chem 2018; Wylie et al. 2017). Asciminib retains activity against ATP-site mutants including T315I; resistance arises via myristoyl-site mutations (sensitive to ATP-competitive TKIs, enabling combinations) and via ABCB1/ABCG2 efflux.

**Drugs as bilayer modifiers.** Ingólfsson & Andersen (Assay Drug Dev Technol 2010; JoVE 2131, 2010) developed a gramicidin-based fluorescence quench assay (ANTS/Tl⁺-loaded LUVs) in which gramicidin channels act as molecular force transducers reporting changes in bilayer properties as experienced by bilayer-spanning proteins. Many amphiphilic drugs alter bilayer elasticity/thickness (hydrophobic mismatch) and thereby modulate membrane protein function nonspecifically—a plausible general mechanism for promiscuous "off-target" effects. A 2020 *J Med Chem* paper extended this into combined in silico + in vitro gramicidin assays for screening bilayer-perturbing liability.

### 2. Experimental and Computational Methods

**Partitioning/thermodynamics.** Equilibrium dialysis and ultracentrifugation with liposomes/LUVs; isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) for membrane–water K (e.g., the lapatinib measurement, using chaotropic co-solvent/DMSO extrapolation to handle poorly soluble drugs); immobilized artificial membrane (IAM) chromatography and liposome electrokinetic chromatography for lipophilicity indices that encode both lipophilic and polar/electrostatic forces (informative for BBB permeation); differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) for drug effects on lipid phase transitions.

**Surface/interfacial.** SPR on L1 chips/supported bilayers; quartz crystal microbalance with dissipation (QCM-D); Langmuir monolayer surface-pressure isotherms; atomic force microscopy; membrane capacitance/electrical measurements of dipole-potential changes.

**Spectroscopy/positioning.** ¹H magic-angle-spinning NOESY cross-relaxation to map drug insertion depth and distribution; ³¹P and ²H NMR order parameters; ¹⁹F ssNMR for drug orientation (e.g., corticosteroids in POPC); paramagnetic relaxation enhancement; fluorescence (Laurdan generalized polarization, DPH anisotropy, FRET) and depth-dependent parallax quenching using brominated or doxyl-labeled lipids; EPR spin labeling. Solid-state NMR reviews explicitly covering kinase inhibitors are now available (Biophys Rev 2025, Huster/Scheidt group; Nat Rev Drug Discov review of ssNMR for membrane-embedded targets).

**Scattering.** Neutron and X-ray reflectometry and small-angle scattering to locate drugs across the bilayer normal.

**Cellular target-engagement/free-drug.** CETSA (cellular thermal shift assay; validated on kinases p38α/MAPK14 and ERK1/2) and CETSA-MS/CETSA-PEA multiplexed readouts; kinobeads/chemical proteomics (Bantscheff et al., Nat Biotechnol 2007, mapping cellular binding of ABL/kinase inhibitors); Kp,uu,cell and fu,cell measurements; intracellular bioavailability Fic. MALDI mass-spectrometry imaging and subcellular fractionation + lipidomics for intracellular/organellar drug distribution.

**Computational.** All-atom and coarse-grained MARTINI MD of permeation/partitioning; umbrella sampling / potential of mean force (PMF) across bilayers, metadynamics, and alchemical free-energy methods. High-throughput CG screens established linear relations between bulk water/octanol partitioning and PMF features (Menichetti & Bereau), and databases of thousands of drug–membrane PMFs now exist (e.g., 15,120 MD simulations across 105 CG solute dimers × six phospholipids, Sci Data 2020). COSMOmic/COSMO-RS thermodynamic approaches; QSAR/QSPR and machine-learning models for permeability and partitioning; MD of EGFR dimers in bilayers, KRAS–RAF at membranes, and drug access to membrane-facing "back pockets." **Pitfalls:** force-field accuracy for ionizable solutes and charged headgroups; sampling of slow flip-flop and protonation-state changes; and reproducing partitioning free energies typically only within ~1–2 kcal/mol (Menichetti/Bereau report ~1.8 kcal/mol reconstruction error), which propagates to large uncertainties in predicted Kp/permeability.

### 3. Efficacy and Resistance Cases

**Biochemical vs cellular potency.** Knight & Shokat (Chem Biol 2005) found biochemical affinities predict cellular activity "to a first approximation," yet many inhibitors deviate—e.g., LY294002 is consistently ~10× more potent in cells than biochemistry predicts. Contributors include cellular ATP competition, phosphatase flux, and—central here—limited intracellular free drug. The cleanest quantitation comes from KAT6A inhibitors: 1.3–260-fold cellular/biochemical potency drop-off, bridged from 73- to 5.4-fold by saturable Kpuu (Trünkle et al., DMD 2020), and the Fic framework demonstrated specifically for kinase inhibitors (Mateus, PNAS 2017).

**Lysosomal sequestration.** Sunitinib (hydrophobic weak base, pKa ≈ 8.95) accumulates in acidic lysosomes by ion trapping. Gotink et al. (*Clin Cancer Res* 2011;17:7337–7346) measured intratumoral sunitinib of 10.9 ± 0.5 µmol/L (mice) and 9.5 ± 2.4 µmol/L (patients) versus ~10-fold lower plasma (1.0 ± 0.1 and 0.3 ± 0.1 µmol/L), with in vitro antiproliferative IC50 of 1.4–2.3 µmol/L; resistant cells showed 1.7–2.5× higher lysosomal sunitinib with unaffected p-Akt/p-ERK, defining lysosomal sequestration as a resistance mechanism. Nintedanib lysosomal sequestration confers resistance in FGFR-driven lung cancer, reversible with bafilomycin A1/chloroquine (Englinger et al., J Exp Clin Cancer Res 2017;36:122). Hydrophobic weak-base drugs trigger TFEB-mediated lysosomal biogenesis, further enhancing entrapment and multidrug resistance (Zhitomirsky & Assaraf). **Controversy:** Skoupá et al. (*Biomolecules* 2019;9:675, PMID 31683643) found that although imatinib, nilotinib, and dasatinib accumulated extensively in lysosomes, sequestration was insufficient to reduce extracellular concentration or affect BCR-ABL signaling—a direct counterpoint arguing lysosomal trapping alone may not drive resistance.

**P-gp/ABC transporter interplay.** The Higgins–Gottesman "hydrophobic vacuum cleaner" model holds that substrates first partition into the bilayer and P-gp (ABCB1) extracts them from the (cytoplasmic) leaflet before spontaneous flip-flop—so membrane partitioning directly governs substrate presentation to the pump; cryo-EM/X-ray structures reveal portals connecting the inner leaflet to the drug-binding cavity. Asciminib is a substrate of both ABCB1 and ABCG2: K562-Dox (ABCB1) and K562-ABCG2 cells showed LD50 of 256 nM and 299 nM respectively versus 24 nM in parental K562 (p < 0.001), with sensitivity restored to 13 nM by cyclosporine and 15 nM by Ko143 (Eadie/Saunders et al., *Oncotarget* 2018, PMID 29568367).

**Cholesterol/rafts.** Raft-localized EGFR correlates with TKI resistance across breast cancer lines, and lovastatin/atorvastatin/MβCD depletion of raft cholesterol sensitizes resistant cells to gefitinib synergistically, restoring p-Akt suppression (Irwin et al., J Cell Physiol 2011). High raft cholesterol reduces gefitinib sensitivity in NSCLC, reversible by cholesterol add-back (Chen et al., J Cell Physiol 2018). A cholesterol/EGFR/Src/ERK/SP1→ERRα axis drives gefitinib and osimertinib resistance, reversed by lovastatin (Mol Cancer 2022). Trastuzumab inhibits EGF-induced raft localization of the EGFR–HER2 heterodimer, and MβCD synergizes to block AKT activation. These establish cholesterol/caveolin-1-dependent membrane organization as a bona fide, pharmacologically actionable resistance axis.

**Lipid remodeling in cancer.** SCD1 and FASN alter membrane fatty-acid saturation; SCD1 activity rises on reoxygenation and supports tumor regrowth/ferroptosis resistance. CPT1C silencing increases plasma-membrane phospholipid saturation and rigidity, reducing doxorubicin uptake and conferring anthracycline resistance (predictive of lower pathological complete response). The general principle—membrane rigidity/saturation lowers passive drug uptake—applies to lipophilic kinase inhibitors and links tumor lipid metabolism to drug exposure.

**Phospholipidosis.** Cationic amphiphilic drugs (CADs), a category into which many basic kinase inhibitors fall, accumulate rapidly in lysosomes, raise lysosomal pH, and cause phospholipidosis (whorled/lamellar "myelin bodies"). Inhibition of lysosomal phospholipase A2 (PLA2G15/LPLA2) is a primary mechanism and predicts drug-induced phospholipidosis (PMID 34087196). Toxicological relevance includes QT prolongation, myopathy, hepatotoxicity, nephrotoxicity, and pulmonary dysfunction—generally reversible on discontinuation, and debated as adaptive vs directly toxic.

**Medicinal chemistry implications.** Deliberate tuning of lipophilicity modulates both membrane affinity and target access; the asciminib story exemplifies exploiting a *lipid-binding* (myristoyl) pocket as an allosteric, mutation-resistant site. Conversely, membrane partitioning correlates with off-target promiscuity/cytotoxicity, so lipophilicity is a two-edged design parameter.

## Recommendations

1. **Measure real membrane partition coefficients rather than relying on logP** (ITC/liposome, IAM chromatography) for any lipophilic/basic kinase inhibitor, and use Kp,uu,cell / fu,cell (or Fic = fu,cell × Kp) to interpret cellular potency. *Threshold to act:* if the cellular/biochemical IC50 gap exceeds ~5–10×, formally decompose it into ATP-competition versus free-drug/access components using a saturable-Kpuu correction before concluding the compound is intrinsically weak in cells.
2. **Characterize the target's membrane microenvironment.** For membrane-proximal targets (EGFR, HER2, RAF, BTK, PI3K), test inhibitor activity under cholesterol depletion/raft disruption and across anionic-lipid-varied model membranes. *Benchmark:* raft cholesterol enrichment or caveolin-1 upregulation in resistant lines should trigger evaluation of a statin/cholesterol-lowering combination, for which preclinical synergy with gefitinib/osimertinib is documented.
3. **Screen for bilayer-modifying/CAD liability early**—gramicidin fluorescence assay, in vitro phospholipidosis readouts, and PLA2G15 inhibition—to flag nonspecific off-target activity and phospholipidosis/toxicology risk before lead optimization commits to high-cLogP scaffolds.
4. **In resistance settings, distinguish membrane-mediated mechanisms** (lysosomal trapping, raft sequestration, P-gp efflux) from target-mutation resistance. Use lysosomotropic agents (chloroquine/bafilomycin) or efflux inhibitors (cyclosporine, Ko143) as mechanistic probes—but treat lysosomal trapping as *adjunctive* rather than a sole driver of clinical resistance pending stronger in vivo/clinical evidence. *Threshold:* pursue lysosome-targeted combinations only where inhibiting sequestration measurably restores target-pathway suppression (e.g., p-ERK/p-Akt), not merely raises intracellular drug.
5. **In computational workflows, combine CG MARTINI PMF screening with all-atom validation and Kpuu-scaled PBPK,** and explicitly flag force-field limits for ionizable solutes; treat predicted partition/permeability values as semi-quantitative (±1–2 kcal/mol ≈ up to ~1–2 log units).

## Caveats
- **Strength of evidence varies by mechanism.** Raft/cholesterol-dependent EGFR resistance and lysosomal sequestration are well documented in vitro, but their causal weight in *clinical* resistance remains debated; the imatinib/nilotinib/dasatinib lysosomal analysis (Skoupá 2019) is a direct, credible counterpoint.
- **Quantitative membrane-partition data for the full TKI panel is sparse.** The strongest hard number is lapatinib (~84,000 M⁻¹ in POPC); a systematic log K table across imatinib, nilotinib, dasatinib, erlotinib, sunitinib, sorafenib, nintedanib, and pazopanib in defined lipid systems was not located and remains an open gap. Existing values should be verified against primary PDFs.
- **Direction of composition effects is drug-species-dependent** (cationic vs neutral): extrapolating "anionic lipids increase binding" universally is incorrect, as the lapatinib data show.
- **Some flagship IC50-gap quantitation is from a non-kinase target** (KAT6A, a lysine acetyltransferase) used as the cleanest exemplar; kinase-specific fold-shifts are more scattered, so the KAT6A and Fic/kinase datasets are best cited together as complementary quantitative backbone.
- **Computational free energies carry ~1–2 kcal/mol uncertainties** that translate into large errors in predicted partition/permeability; MD-derived numbers should be treated as semi-quantitative and cross-checked experimentally.
- **RAF "paradoxical activation"** is driven primarily by inhibitor-induced dimerization/RAS-on membrane recruitment and allosteric autoinhibition relief; a distinct, direct inhibitor–membrane interaction has not been cleanly separated from these protein-level effects and should not be overclaimed.
